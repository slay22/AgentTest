# The Anti-AI Backlash Has a Reason

Merriam-Webster picked "slop" as its 2025 Word of the Year. That's not a bug — it's the public naming what it actually experienced.

## The polls flipped

In 2021, 37% of Americans said they were more concerned than excited about AI. A Pew Research Center survey in June 2026 puts that number at 52%, with only 9% more excited than concerned. Among adults 18 to 29, a majority (55%) is now more concerned than excited, up from 31% five years earlier. The U.S. is one of the most AI-pessimistic countries in the world, and the people most likely to be building the next model are the ones who least want one.

The split isn't partisan. Gallup's 2026 Bentley-Gallup survey found trust in AI companies fell from 30% to 20% among 18-to-29-year-olds in a single year, and from 41% to 35% among Republicans — the group that had been the most trusting. Pew's September 2025 work shows the two parties have effectively converged in the middle of the skepticism.

## The reasons are concrete, not vibes

A Towards Data Science analysis of the polling argues the negative opinions are "largely driven by negative personal experiences with AI" — the chatbot that gave a wrong answer, the feed that got worse, the essay the student submitted. That's the slop problem, and it's real. Simon Willison wrote the May 2024 blog post that named it, drawing the analogy to spam, and Merriam-Webster crowned it 2025's word of the year. A Common Sense Media study found that three-quarters of US teens use AI companions, and a July 2025 study put the share of teens who used one for homework or moral questions at a similar level.

The second reason is material. Data Center Watch found that in Q2 2025 alone, local opposition blocked or delayed $98 billion in data-center projects across the U.S. In Q1 2026 the count was 75 projects worth roughly $130 billion. The complaints aren't abstract: utility bills, water draw, noise, property values. In Indianapolis, a packed city-county council room cheered when Google withdrew its 468-acre rezoning petition in September 2025. A March 2025 Harvard Law School paper by Ari Peskoe argued that the public, not the tech companies, is effectively paying for the new power plants and transmission lines that data centers require.

The third reason is labor. The 2023 WGA strike made generative AI its central issue, and the resulting contract set terms for how studios can use the technology. In February 2025, more than 1,000 UK musicians — including Kate Bush, Annie Lennox, and Damon Albarn — released a silent protest album, *Is This What We Want?*, to oppose the UK government's plan to let AI companies train on copyrighted music without a licence. The album reached No. 38 on the UK charts. In March 2026, an unlikely coalition of MAGA Republicans, democratic socialists, labor activists, and church leaders signed a Pro-Human AI Declaration arguing that AI should serve people, not replace them.

## The movement is diffuse, not unified

What looks like "anti-AI" from the outside is actually at least four different arguments happening at once. The AI-safety crowd, organized around groups like Pause AI, wants a pause on frontier development. The data-center crowd wants moratoria on construction near their towns. The creative-sector crowd wants copyright opt-in and consent. The labor crowd wants contract protections. They share a target, but their asks don't overlap.

The largest single protest to date was a few hundred people, not thousands. On February 28, 2026, Pause AI and Pull the Plug organized a march through London's King's Cross — past the UK offices of OpenAI, Meta, and Google DeepMind — to demand the CEOs publicly support a pause. In San Francisco, a similar march hit the same three companies in July 2026. In September 2025, an 18-day hunger strike outside Google DeepMind's London office drew international attention, and Demis Hassabis said he'd support a pause "in principle."

## What the industry is doing about it

The honest answer is not much, yet. Zuckerberg acknowledged "low-quality content, aka AI slop" as a concern but ruled out making judgments about what should be allowed. In March 2026, the Trump administration secured a pledge from AI company executives to cover the energy costs of their data centers by building or buying from new power plants — a direct response to the utility-bill complaint. Anthropic removed a commitment to pause if its own AI became too dangerous from the third version of its Responsible Scaling Policy, which is exactly the kind of move that keeps the safety crowd mobilized.

The industry's messaging problem is that it keeps talking about capability — the next model, the next benchmark, the next breakthrough — while the public is talking about cost, consent, and control. Those aren't the same conversation, and until they are, the polls aren't going to move.

The backlash isn't a reflex. It's the public doing the math on a technology it was told to be excited about, and finding the bill came due.
